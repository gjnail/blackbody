# Wood

Wood is fifteen materials: pine (Wood), spruce, Douglas fir, oak, ash, maple, birch, walnut, cherry, mahogany, teak, cedar and balsa, and two made from wood, plywood and MDF. Each is its own wood all through: its density, how stiff and how strong it is along its grain and across it (from the USDA Wood Handbook), and how it looks.

## How it looks

A trunk grows a ring a year round its pith: pale earlywood in spring, darker, denser latewood in summer, then the next ring starts at once. The stage draws that, solid through the object, so a cut, a break or a bullet's hole shows the same wood inside:

- **Grain.** A board's grain runs along its longest side (a log's along its axis). A board is sawn from the trunk off its middle and a little off its axis, so its wide face cuts the rings at a slant: flat-sawn boards show long nested arches ("cathedrals"), quarter-sawn ones (about one in four) straight lines; its edge shows straight lines, its end arcs of rings round a pith off to one side.
- **Rings** wander (no trunk is round) and widen and narrow year by year; softwoods (pine, spruce, fir, cedar) turn abruptly from earlywood to latewood, hardwoods gradually.
- **Knots**, where branches grew out of the pith: dark ovals with rings of their own, the grain swirling round them (pine has the most).
- **Pores and rays**: oak and ash have rows of open vessels in their earlywood, dark dashes along the grain; oak's broad rays show as silvery flecks where a face cuts them square. Maple and walnut have figure, a ripple in the grain's sheen.
- **Heartwood** near the pith is darker than the sapwood outside it. **End grain** is darker and rougher.
- **Plywood**'s edges show its plies, light and dark with glue lines between; **MDF** is a felt of fibres, no grain at all.

Pick one in an object's *Material*; *Own colour* stains it (the grain stays).

## How it breaks

Wood is strong along its grain and weak across it, and splits along it far more easily than it breaks across it. Made breakable (*Breaks into: Splinters*, which is what a wooden object gets), it is cut along its grain into bundles of fibres, and each bundle into lengths, the cuts between lengths staggered from bundle to bundle and slanted. Lengths of a bundle hold each other as the fibres do; bundles side by side hold each other as the wood does across its grain, less the wider their face (a split runs as a crack). So:

- A board bent until it breaks does not snap square: its bundles break one by one at their own weak points along it and peel apart along the grain, a jagged greenstick break with splinters standing out of both halves. A 19 mm pine board on a 30 cm span shrugs off a kilogram dropped on it and snaps in two under 5 kg dropped half a metre; oak takes more, balsa less.
- A log (a cylinder) is cut into wedges round its pith, in lengths: it checks and splits along its rays.
- A bullet holes a board going in and tears a split out of its back along the grain, splinters flying (see [Bullets](bullets.md)).
- It burns as wood does (*Burnable*), floats as its density says (balsa high, oak low).

## Presets

- *Breaking a board*: a 10 kg weight dropped from a metre onto a pine board across two blocks, slowed down four times.
- *Bullets through boards*: a 9 mm pistol and a .308 rifle into six pine boards stood 2.5 cm apart: the pistol stops in the last, the rifle goes through all six.
- *Woods*: every wood side by side, as boards and as logs.

## Limits

- A falling axe or wedge cannot bite into end grain (things that fall are rigid), so a log struck on its end with one cracks along its rays but does not come apart in two; struck from the side, or by a blast or a bullet, it splits.
- Wood does not bend before it breaks: a plank over a gap stays straight until it snaps.
